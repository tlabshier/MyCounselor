# 260921 – Lessons Not Passed to the Next Generation

# The Mother of the Bride


Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 21 September 2026


*A counseling story. The person in this story is real, but every identifying detail has been changed, and the conversation has been rearranged into a case study. If you recognize yourself in it, it is because the story is common, not because it is about you.*


---


## How it began


The woman I was speaking with — I will call her Ellen — had just married off her eldest daughter. She had carried three roles at once that day: mother of the bride, matron of honor, and event planner. By evening she was wrung out. It had been a beautiful day, she said, and a very long one, and far more emotional than she had expected.


That last phrase is the kind of thing a counselor learns to hear. "More emotional than I expected" means something was under the wedding that wasn't the wedding. So I asked whether it was the ordinary grief of letting a daughter go into a new phase of life.


She paused, thought, and then told me the real story.


## The story


Ellen's marriage had ended some years earlier. Her former husband was rigid and judgmental. She had spent fifteen years accommodating him, then another five trying to untangle what those fifteen years had built. The untangling failed, and the marriage ended.


Her daughter had grown up inside that household. Ellen had talked with her a great deal over the last several years about what she had been through — carefully, without trying to turn the girl against her father — and she believed her daughter understood.


Then the daughter chose a husband. And in the months before the wedding, Ellen began to see things she recognized. She saw emotional immaturity in the young man. More than that, she saw a pattern in how the two of them interacted that looked, to her, exactly like the pattern she had lived inside for twenty years. One moment in particular she could not stop thinking about. When the young man became hard or critical, her daughter did the same thing she had done as a child when her father was hard or critical: she tuned out. She went somewhere inside herself for a minute. She did not push back, did not name what was happening, did not even appear to see it.


"It's hard to see yourself," Ellen said. "It's hard to be objective."


She wanted me to understand that this was not a case of a mother trying to stop a bad marriage. The young man was not a terrible person. If he had been, she said, she would have done everything in her power to stop the wedding. She had made sure her daughter knew there was always a way out before the vows. What she was seeing was subtler than villainy: habits and dynamics which, if never addressed, would lead to disaster, and which are far easier to address in the first year than to untangle decades later.


The weeks before the wedding were consumed by regret. What if she had done things differently? What if she had stood up to her own husband on the first day instead of the fifteenth year? Could she have kept her daughter from walking the same road?


She hadn't laid all of this on her daughter on the wedding day. But on the drive up, the two of them had a good conversation, and Ellen told her daughter what she had come to: that she was letting go of the idea that she could control the outcome, that she had to trust her daughter's own judgment, and that her daughter's path was her own — for better or worse, whether she repeated the same lessons the same hard way, or lived through similar things with a much better ending.


"I just didn't realize that letting go would encompass so many things," she said.


## What I saw


Two people are in this story, and only one was in the room. So the first thing to sort out is which one the counseling is for.


Ellen's account of her daughter is probably accurate, and it describes something I've seen over and over in thirty years of practice: the person who walks straight into familiar pain and cannot see it. If we were actually awake to what we were doing, we would not do it. That is very nearly a definition of pathology. Ellen had put her finger on the precise mechanism — the tuning out, the going inside, the not-seeing. That dynamic describes a person either with a pathology in progress or a pathology waiting to happen. Such a person has no defense against what they cannot perceive.


Some people learn by precept. You can tell them, and they are wise, and they change course. Most people learn by experience. Some learn by precept. Ellen had taught by precept for years, and her daughter had understood — with her head. But the body plays out its emotional stories on its own schedule. The heart and the mind can be years apart. What Ellen was watching was the gap between the two.


The obvious dynamic was that her daughter had married her father. That is not a criticism of the daughter. It is nearly a law. The patterns she never worked out with the first man in her life are still there, unfinished, and something in the soul goes looking for the place where the work can be completed. She did not choose the pattern; the pattern chose the husband. Now the pins are all set up, and we are going to watch the ball roll. Most people don't get a strike and clear out all their pathologies and ignorance on the first encounter with their developmental deficits and errors. Most people leave a spare standing, and they deal with it for as long as it takes. Many never overcome their errors in behavioral programming because they don't know what they don't know.


None of that was news to Ellen. She had already come to it herself on the drive to the wedding. Which told me the daughter was not the patient.


## The real diagnosis


The patient was the mother, and her problem wasn't her daughter's marriage. Her problem was that she was in the middle of a passage.


A passage — a real transition from one phase of life to another — has a particular cruelty built into it. Everything that animated you during the old phase, all the feeling that let you live and cope and act inside it, is suddenly of no use. And in Ellen's case the old phase did not end cleanly. It was not "we finished, well done, on to the next thing." It ended with the work incomplete. For years she had been in what I called mom mode: the trainer, the one who directs, warns, teaches, passes on wisdom, and tries to spare her child what she herself had suffered. She was fully charged for that job. Then the job ended mid-sentence, and the charge had nowhere to go.


This is the thing that gives people ulcers. The body is fully activated for an action that the situation no longer permits. You cannot go to your married daughter and say, here is how to think, here is what to do, try this, don't do that. The gate is closed. So you sit there, activated and paralyzed, and it comes out as sleeplessness, as rumination, as regret.


Ellen recognized the pattern. "I'm now understanding what people must go through as empty nesters." She had never expected that to be a problem for her; she has other children at home and more projects than she can finish in a lifetime. But she saw that it was never about having too much time. It is about emotional redirection. It's an intangible goal, which makes it hard to address.


## The sleep


Once we had the diagnosis, the rest of her symptoms fell into place. Ellen had been sleeping four to four and a half hours a night for weeks. The night before we spoke, a friend had given her a sedative, and she had taken half of it and finally slept — and she hated it. She did not want to be on medication or on a shelf of supplements just to sleep. And she added something: this was not new. She had struggled with sleep since she was a small child. She remembered lying awake all night on family road trips, worrying.


So worry was her issue, and had been for a lifetime. That is not an accusation. Worry is a capability of the human frame; we all have it. The question is never how to stop worrying, because you can't. The question is: when is worry useful? There is a time for everything. When is the time for this?


My answer, from my own life as much as my practice: worry is a heightened state of mental and emotional arousal, and heightened arousal is extremely good for creativity. So do not fight it. Aim it. If you find yourself awake and worried, get up and use the state. Journal, stream of consciousness. Make the list. Put a sticky note on the wall for every thought that comes. Sit in meditation and see what solutions surface. Or pray — and I mean pray as though something will die if it does not change, because in my experience that is the only kind of prayer that moves anything. People sneer at the foxhole believer, but they should emulate him. When life gives you urgent or insoluble problems, you need a miracle, and you should pray for one like it's important - because it is. After praying, go out and act full out, because prayer is not asking God to do it for you. Prayer is asking for the setup, the alignment of circumstances, that you then walk into with your whole effort and bring a righteous solution.


On the sleep itself, I told her what I tell most patients: sleep when you are tired. I have slept three to four hours a night for decades, then worked, then napped for an hour when my body asked for it, and my health has not suffered. An entire continent takes a siesta. We think there is something wrong with waking at three in the morning because our culture is built on an eight-hour block and doesn't allow for anything else. If you wake up, you're awake; get up and do something until you are actually sleepy. Worrying about sleeping doesn't solve the problem; doing something toward solving it does.


She said this alone set her mind at ease in a way the sedative had not.


## A word about feelings and faith


One more thread explained why Ellen had spent fifteen years accommodating a harsh man before she began to resist.


She had been taught, as a Christian wife, that she could not trust what she felt. Her feelings were to be set aside; her job was to do what she was told a wife does. Her former husband still regards her, she said, as something of a heretic for acknowledging her emotions at all instead of following blindly. And she asked me an honest question: when she does follow what she feels, how does she know whether it is the Spirit leading her or just her own emotions?


I told her that what she had described was a diagnosis of the state of the church, not of her. The path of setting aside all feeling and simply obeying is the path of the renunciant — the monk, the yogi, the sadhu, the Buddha. You can find verses to prove this solution is Biblical, but this life is not the fullness of the life in Christ. I believe a life lived with deeply felt emotion is the most satisfying. Without emotion, life is data. It is a series of facts with no significance, and emotions give it meaning and juice. Any teaching that tells a person to ignore, dismiss, or suppress their emotions is not describing an abundant life; it is describing a way to make the person easier to manage. Sometimes emotions must be noted and set aside to take action, but as a habit of life, it leads to meaninglessness, a deadening of the spirit, or a buildup of tension that will express itself in another way.


As for telling the Spirit from her own feelings: judge it rightly. Take a straight moral inventory. Are you living in the fruit of the Spirit — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control — and are the things vexing you actually the absence of those fruits in what is being done to you? Then your feelings are giving you accurate information. There is a simpler test still: would you recommend what you are going through to everybody? Would you say, yes, every wife should live this way? If the answer is no, your feeling that something isn't right, and something must change, is confirmed.


## The change


Diagnosis is only the second step. The third is change, and here is the change I gave her.


Emotions have no steering wheel, no brakes, and no gas pedal that you can see. They are intangible. So the whole work of a passage is to find out where the controls for your emotions actually are, because they exist.


The steering wheel: Ellen has other children. The trainer's charge, built for her eldest, can be turned deliberately toward the ones still at home. Everything she learned in this hard season — every warning she wishes she could have delivered, every piece of wisdom she gathered — is now available for them. That is not a consolation prize. It is where the energy goes.


The brakes: when she notices too much intensity, effort, and worry pouring into the one place where she cannot act, she can use awareness as a signal to consciously stop the worry and focus her action somewhere she can act: prayer, journaling, a solution, a task, an errand, serving someone. This is not a brake that turns motion into wasted heat. It is a regenerative brake, like the one in an electric car: it captures momentum, stores it, and uses it later for useful work.


The rule that makes it work: you have to steer the car in a new direction. You cannot say, "I'll just do something else instead," and get into a different car. That is distraction, and distraction does not drain the charge. The move is: "I am feeling this. I am taking this feeling — all the passion, all the interest, all the spiritual drive behind it — and I am pointing it here." If the pressure is intense, you up your game to match it. You find more outlets. You spend yourself so completely on what you can do that there is nothing left over to spend. The exhaustion of complete service, hard work, pushing fully in another direction, knowing that you are using the energy not directed toward the object of care, but dedicating that passion in a new direction, allows the energy to drain consciously. Remembering that you cannot do the thing you missed, but you can make the world better in another related way, allows you to discharge the passion. 


Ellen said, "So, just being very intentional about where the emotion is going." And yes, that is exactly what I was recommending.


## A note on method


For thirty years, counseling was the part of my practice I loved most. I had no mental health degree, although I took all the counseling classes that were offered in my Naturopathic program, and counseling was one of the modalities we were expected to use. People typically came to me for other reasons. Somewhere in a thorough, personal intake, they would mention the part of their life that wasn't working, and I would ask whether they wanted to talk about it. Then I listened — usually for half an hour to an hour without saying much beyond a mirroring question — until I saw the whole picture. When I saw it, I knew what to say.


I started out with the secular idea that a counselor's job is to hear people, accept them, and that acceptance itself produces change. The Rogerian listening therapy is very powerful, but it has limitations. I got results only when I understood that counseling often requires confronting the belief, the habit, the attitude, the error of action, or the wrong life perspective. After that, the challenge is guiding them through the gradient of change. But before change can happen, the first step is rightly judging the error, getting agreement and acknowledgment of the diagnosis. And that must be delivered in a way they can hear it: tentative confrontation, "It seems like this is what is going on; does that sound right?" By saying it in a way the person can hear, they become my ally against the pattern that is making their life run badly. Once you've identified the problem and gotten agreement on it, you can take willful action toward change.


The counseling cycle, and all of life models it, has three steps.


- Rapport: find out what is actually going on.

- Confrontation: name it, and reach agreement that it is real.

- Change: do the work of developing new habits of thoughts, speech, and action.


Everything else is technique, and there are a thousand techniques. But one bottom line underlies them all, and I said it to Ellen because it applies to her daughter's marriage as much as to her own passage. Whether you are the one behaving badly or the one being treated badly, the only thing available to any of us is to act out the fruit of the Spirit. We never get a holiday from it. We never get to say, "I've had enough, now it's my turn to act badly." That does not feel fair. It is nonetheless the fact, and it is why we admire actors so much: it is a very high calling to act well in the face of feelings that pull the other way.


Ellen's daughter has a spare standing. Ellen cannot knock it down for her. But Ellen can steer her own car, and she left the conversation knowing where the wheel was.
